# Module 0x: The Exposure Lab
### Public by Design — Feeling What "Public" Actually Means

**Placement:** Early in the semester (Week 2 or 3), before students commit
any real coursework to their private repos. Pairs well with readings on
surveillance, data permanence, or the "right to be forgotten."

**Format:** Individual, ungraded or pass/fail on completion only.
No content quality is assessed — only that the exercise was completed
and reflected on.

---

## Learning objective

Students experience, firsthand and at low stakes, what it means for
something to be public on the internet: indexable, forkable, cacheable,
and effectively permanent — before they ever have to decide how to
handle their own real coursework under that same set of rules.

## Setup (instructor, before the module opens)

- Confirm this is fully opt-in and clearly separated from graded work.
  Include a one-paragraph consent notice in the assignment prompt
  explaining exactly what will become public and why.
- Provide a short list of safe, low-stakes content ideas so no student
  improvises with something they'll later regret (see "Content ideas"
  below).
- Do **not** require students to use any account linked to their real
  identity, transcripts, or existing coursework.

## Student instructions

**Step 1 — Create a burner identity.**
Sign up for a new, free GitHub account not connected to your real name,
school email, or existing coursework. Use a throwaway email address
(most email providers let you create aliases, or use a disposable-email
service). This account exists only for this lab and should never be
linked back to you.

**Step 2 — Publish something small and public.**
Create a new **public** repository under this burner account. Add one
short file — a paragraph, a list, a tiny script, whatever you like —
that says something true but unremarkable about yourself (a hobby, an
opinion about a movie, a made-up fact you're comfortable being public).
Nothing you'd mind a stranger reading. Nothing identifying.

Content ideas if you're stuck:
- A ranked list of your five favorite snacks
- A short review of the last thing you watched
- A tiny script that prints a joke

**Step 3 — Watch it become findable.**
Over the next 3–5 days, check the following and record what you find in
your reflection:
- Search your repo's exact file content in a search engine. Is it indexed
  yet?
- Check whether [archive.org's Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org)
  has crawled it, or submit it manually and note what "saving" it there
  actually means for permanence.
- Look at your repo's **Insights → Traffic** tab (if visible on your
  account tier) to see if anyone besides you has viewed it.

**Step 4 — Try (and fail) to fully take it back.**
Delete the repository. Then search for it again. Note what you find —
cached versions, forks other people may have made in the interim (check
your repo's fork count before deleting), or nothing at all. Either
result is useful data.

## Reflection (submitted privately, to instructor only)

Answer in ~300–500 words, submitted to your **private** course repo
(not the burner account):

1. What surprised you about how quickly, or slowly, your content became
   findable or archived?
2. When you tried to delete it, what actually disappeared and what
   didn't?
3. This was something trivial you chose freely. How would this feel
   different if the content were a graded assignment, a mistake, or
   something you were required to publish rather than chose to?
4. Where else in your digital life have you already generated public,
   permanent artifacts without going through a moment like this one?

## Instructor notes

- This is intentionally decoupled from any real coursework or identity —
  the pedagogical payload is the *experience* of publicness, not the
  content itself.
- Some students may already understand this intellectually; the value is
  still in the felt experience, and their reflection will likely surface
  that gap explicitly.
- Good discussion-section follow-up: ask who found their repo indexed
  and who didn't, and unpack why that's unpredictable and outside their
  control — that unpredictability is itself the point.
- If a student expresses discomfort at any point, let them stop and
  delete without penalty. The lab's value doesn't require finishing it.
