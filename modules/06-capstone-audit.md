## 🧠 1. The Idea

All semester we have asked the same question about different systems: who holds the power here, and who pays for it? The capstone asks you to answer that question about one real system of your choosing, using the frameworks you already know.

There is no new required reading. Your main framework is **Ivan Illich**, whom you have read in Modules 1, 2, and 3. You will add at least one other framework from the course, check your claims against real evidence, and take a position of your own.

The final window is short (Dec 8 to Dec 15), so the choosing and the first research happen earlier, at the two checkpoints below.

### A reminder about Illich's three ideas

* **The two watersheds.** At the first watershed, a tool starts to do real good for the people who use it. At the second, the tool has grown to the point where it serves the institution that runs it more than the people it was for, and it starts to cause the problems it was meant to solve.
* **Radical monopoly.** This does **not** mean a company is big or popular, or that people depend on a product. It means one kind of system has taken over how a basic need is met, so that doing without it is no longer a real choice. Illich's example is the car: once a city is built around driving, walking stops being an option. The test is whether a person can realistically opt out.
* **Convivial tools.** A convivial tool is one that people can use for their own purposes, as much or as little as they choose, without needing permission or an expert, and without their use getting in the way of anyone else's.

## 🔍 2. The Audit

Choose one real system that is in use today and that affects people's lives or work. Then take these five steps.

1. **Choose and frame the system.** Say what it does, who uses it, who is affected by it, and why it matters. Surprising choices are welcome. You may return to a system you wrote about in a lab or discussion, but the audit has to go well beyond what you said there.
2. **Locate the watershed.** Has this system crossed Illich's second watershed? If so, when and how: what changed between the tool serving its users and the tool managing them? Then apply the radical monopoly test: can a person realistically opt out, and what does it cost them if they do? It is fine to conclude that the system has *not* crossed the watershed or is *not* a radical monopoly, as long as you show why.
3. **Find the convivial alternative.** Identify a real or plausible alternative that does the *same job* but leaves more control with the people who use it. Compare the two: who gains, who loses, and what does each one cost? If you want a model, Cory Doctorow's post [*"The technopolitics of ad-tech"*](https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/19/spying-v-context/) compares surveillance advertising with contextual advertising in just this way.
4. **Test your claims against the evidence.** Use O'Neil's three tests (opacity, scale, damage) to check what you have argued so far. What can outsiders actually see of how the system works? How many people does it reach? What harm can you document, and to whom? Use at least three independent sources, and say so when they disagree or when you could not confirm something. You may build a small prototype (a script, a simulation, a mock-up) if it helps you test a claim, but it is supporting evidence. The written argument is the deliverable.
5. **Bring in a second framework, take a position, and answer the best objection.** Choose at least one other framework from a different module (Franklin, Watters, Fogg, O'Neil, Foucault, Innis, Dreyfus, *Magnifica Humanitas*, or another course reading). Look for a place where it pulls *against* Illich when applied to your system. For example, Franklin's prescriptive technologies can be efficient and safe in ways Illich gives little credit for. Then say clearly what you think should be done about this system, and answer the **strongest** objection you can find, not a weak one.

## 📖 5. Optional: A Second Angle from Langdon Winner

This section is optional. Nothing in the rubric requires it.

If you want another way into Step 2, read **Langdon Winner**, *"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"* (*Daedalus*, 1980) — [Read the essay (PDF)](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~beki/cs4001/Winner.pdf). Illich asks whether a tool has grown past the point where it serves people. Winner asks a different question: **where does the politics of a system actually live?** Is it in a design choice that could have gone another way, or in something the system cannot run without? A design choice can be redesigned. A built-in requirement may mean rejecting the system altogether.

Doctorow's ad-tech post (linked in Step 3) builds on Winner and adds one more question you may take up if you wish: when a design can't be changed, can users, builders, or regulators create a **countermeasure**? If you propose one, say what it would change, what it would leave in place, and who it would fail.

If you use Winner, he can count as your second framework in Step 5.

<details>
<summary>📚 Reading guide: Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?"</summary>

The essay is about 14 pages and dense, so here is a map. Read it yourself first, then use this to check your understanding.

**The question.** Can machines, structures, and systems be judged for the forms of power and authority they embody, and not just for how efficient or safe they are?

**Claim 1: Technical arrangements as forms of order.** The design of a specific device or system settles an issue within a community, and it could have been designed differently. Winner's examples include the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways (see the caution below), the broad boulevards of nineteenth-century Paris, molding machines introduced during a labor dispute at a factory in the 1880s, and the mechanical tomato harvester. He is careful to say the harvester was not part of a plot. Its effects came from a process that was biased toward certain interests.

**Claim 2: Inherently political technologies.** A technical system *requires*, or is strongly compatible with, particular social relationships. Winner separates a system that *requires* certain conditions to operate (his main example is nuclear power) from one that is merely *compatible* with certain arrangements (his example is solar energy).

**What to watch for.** The line between "requires" and "strongly favors" is hard to draw. Notice where Winner draws it, and where you might draw it differently for your system.

**A caution about the Long Island example.** It is Winner's best-known illustration, and it comes from Robert Caro's biography of Robert Moses. Historians have challenged how well the story can be established. See Bernward Joerges's article *"Do Politics Have Artefacts?"* (*Social Studies of Science*, 1999), which was written as a response to Winner — [free copy via SSOAR (PDF)](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/bitstream/handle/document/33570/ssoar-ssos-1999-3-joerges-Do_Politics_have_Artefacts.pdf?sequence=1). Judging how strong the evidence is for what a design was *meant* to do is part of the work of an audit.

</details>
