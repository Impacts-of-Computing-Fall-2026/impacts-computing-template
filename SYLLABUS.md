## 🗺️ 1. Course Description & Working Philosophy

### Institutional Course Description

This course considers the ethical and social impacts of contemporary technology with an emphasis on the impact of computing, especially on children (e.g., how modern-day information technologies affect our privacy, how social relations are affected, how ethical decisions will need to be made, etc.). Topics to be discussed include: ethics, social media, personal safety, reproductive and human enhancement technologies, information technologies, artificial intelligence, technology, and the environment.

### Our Core Philosophical Friction

Traditional tech frameworks operate on the working thesis that technology is a morally neutral tool that can be used well simply by understanding what it promotes or discourages. This course serves as a direct, critical investigation of that thesis. Drawing from **Ursula Franklin**, we analyze technology not as a neutral checklist of tools, but as an active, *prescriptive practice* that enforces political hierarchy, compliance, and social control. 

To guide our alternative path, we ground our design in the theories of social philosopher **Ivan Illich**. In his seminal text *Tools for Conviviality*, Illich warns against "manipulative tools"—technologies built by an elite class that strip users of autonomy, track behaviors, and enforce dependency. In contrast, this course treats your local computer workstation terminal as a **convivial tool**: an accessible, transparent utility designed to maximize your individual liberty, resist corporate capture, and preserve your data sovereignty.

This repository operates entirely outside commercial, tracking-heavy Educational Technology (EdTech) spaces. We do not use proprietary Learning Management System dashboards for our weekly cycles. As **Audrey Watters** warns, traditional corporate learning dashboards function as Skinnerian teaching machines engineered to measure click telemetry and enforce behavioral compliance. 

Instead, we embrace **Minimal Computing**—a technical design philosophy that strips away software bloat, tracking cookies, and high-bandwidth dependencies to actively fight **Digital Redlining**. By working exclusively in plain-text Markdown via local Git terminal environments, we reject manipulative interfaces and execute an act of *technological ascesis*. We guarantee a learning space that manifests what Illich mapped out as an autonomous, peer-to-peer **"Learning Web"**—low-bandwidth, decentralized, deeply private, and accessible to anyone regardless of hardware access limitations.

To guide our technical audits, we adopt the cognitive split defined in the 2025 Vatican doctrinal note ***Antiqua et nova*** (§14). We recognize that computational networks excel at **Ratio** (discursive, mathematical optimization), but possess zero **Intellectus** (the intuitive, embodied human conscience). Throughout this term, your work will be an active defense of *intellectus* against the modern corporate urge to flatten human life into a black-box data matrix.

## 🎯 2. Course Learning Objectives

By the end of this semester, students will be able to:

1. **Philosophical Argumentation:** Understand the basics of philosophical argumentation.
2. **Ethical Reasoning:** Understand basic approaches to ethical and moral reasoning and apply principles of moral reasoning to difficult ethical questions.

## 🎭 3. The Privacy Model & Class Covenant

To protect your personal data from commercial collection and from casual exposure to classmates or the public, every student works in their **own private GitHub repository**. Only you and course staff can see it — it is not a fork, it is not public, and it will not appear in any search engine.

* You may choose a class handle (your real first name, a nickname, or a pseudonym) to attach to your commits, separate from any personal email address. Setup instructions are in the Student Guide.
* Your real-world identity is known only to course staff, via the private class roster — never exposed through the repository itself.
* All weekly submissions, discussion posts, and technical assignments are private to you and course staff by default, unless a specific assignment explicitly asks you to make something public (see the opt-in **Exposure Lab**, Module 0x).

## 📈 4. Evaluation, Grading, & Dual Submission Formats

Your final grade will be computed across four clear operational vectors:

| Assessment Vector           | Weight  | Operational Requirement                                      |
| :-------------------------- | :------ | :----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Read / View / Annotate**  | **25%** | Deep engagement with text, videos, and documentation via local markdown summaries and mind maps. |
| **Interact / Engage**       | **20%** | Substantive and meaningful discussion interactions in our shared `class-commons` GitHub Discussions space. |
| **Ethical & Code Analyses** | **30%** | Comprehensive forensic submissions running tests against production software models and policies. |
| **Final Capstone Audit**    | **25%** | An open-notes deep-dive forensic audit analyzing real-world systemic algorithmic scenarios. |

### Choosing Your Submission Format

Every week, you will choose **one** of two submission formats for your lab work. You may freely switch between them from week to week based on your goals. (Note: this is a different choice from the Desktop-vs-command-line setup decision in the Student Guide — that's about which tool you use to submit; this is about the *kind* of submission you produce.)

* **The Developer Format:** Built for students pursuing hard algorithmic implementation and engineering diagnostics. Submissions require functional code execution scripts (`.py`, `.sh`, `.js`), dataset distribution verifications, and plain-text inline logic annotations.
* **The Analyst Format:** Built for students pursuing structural policy deconstruction, UX interface forensics, and socio-technical policy critique. Submissions require deeply structured analytical reports (`LAB-SUBMISSION.md`) checking live systems against our philosophical frameworks.

### 🧪 The Laboratory Sandbox Operational Registry

This repository contains four custom minimal-computing software simulators located in the `labs/` path directory. To complete your weekly lab submissions, you will run these localized interactive scripts to generate your own empirical raw tracking logs:

* `labs/02-nudge-simulation.html`: Simulates automated tracking triggers to evaluate Audrey Watters' behaviorist compliance critiques.
* `labs/03-bias-simulation.html`: Generates data queues to demonstrate how micro-biasing vectors systematically evade macro-compliance checkers.
* `labs/04-surveillance-simulation.html`: Illustrates how tracking logic independently emerges from un-auditable, optimization-focused code.
* `labs/05-alchemy-simulation.html`: Runs scenario logic splits to verify Dreyfus's alchemical boundaries and category error critiques.

## ⚙️ 8. Repository Operation & Course Policies

### Code & Communication Etiquette

Class discussion happens in **GitHub Discussions**, inside our shared `class-commons` repository. When communicating there or via repository issues, maintaining a professional and respectful environment is mandatory.

- **Email Format:** When emailing your instructor, please use your Mville e-mail address and identify yourself by full name and course number. Place the course number, followed by the subject of the e-mail, in the subject line. Failure to adhere to these guidelines may result in a delayed response or no response at all.
- **Disagreement & Debate:** Classmate perspectives and opinions should be respected, even if they differ from one's own positions. Disagreements, debates, and other contentious dialogue are welcomed, but must be carried out in a manner that focuses on finding better answers, not making personal attacks. Disruptive behavior will not be tolerated.

### Late Commit & Make-Up Policy

Because this is an asynchronous repository structure where peer evaluation and code reviews rely on timely submissions, extensions are not offered. Only coursework completed on time is eligible for full credit. Exceptions are processed exclusively under the following situations, provided that the instructor is notified in advance:

1. **Athletic / Sponsored Trips:** Travel dates accompanied by a signed memo from the coach must be presented to the instructor prior to travel dates.
2. **Religious Observances:** Advanced notice must be submitted to the instructor.
3. **Extenuating Circumstances:** Includes family emergencies, severe illnesses, or accidents. Students will work with SHAC to document such circumstances.
